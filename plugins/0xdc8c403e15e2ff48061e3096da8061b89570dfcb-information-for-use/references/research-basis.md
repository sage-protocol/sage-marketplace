# Research Basis — Information for Use

## ISO 24495-1:2023 (Plain language — Part 1: Governing principles and guidelines)

From the contents and scope of ISO 24495-1:2023:

1. **Relevant:** Readers get what they need.
2. **Findable:** Readers can find what they need.
3. **Understandable:** Readers can understand what they find.
4. **Usable:** Readers can use the information.

ISO 24495-1 applies to technical writing as well as documents for the general public, and to printed or digital information that is primarily text. Principle 1 guideline titles in DIN ISO 24495-1:2024-03: identify the readers, identify their purpose, identify the context, select the document type, select the content readers need.

## IEC/IEEE 82079-1:2019 & ISO/IEC/IEEE 26514:2022

IEC/IEEE 82079-1:2019 (from the official abstract and IEEE scope statement): information for use is necessary for safe use and helpful for efficient and effective use. "Information for use" is the generic term; "instructions for use" is a synonym; step-by-step text is a subset. Empirical evaluation is in the normative part.

ISO/IEC/IEEE 26514:2022 (from the ISO abstract): software information for users has requirements for structure, content, and format, developed from what users need and how the information will be presented and maintained.

## Write for the actual audience, not a readability formula

- **U.S. Digital.gov plain-language principles** (`https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles`): write for the actual audience. Do not write down to an 8th-grade class unless the readers are an 8th-grade class. Use a topic sentence, active voice, a summary before a long document, tables where they carry the information, and lists where detail would otherwise overload the reader.
- **Jones et al. 2012** (DOI 10.2190/TW.42.4.b, abstract): pronouns affected inferential comprehension of environmental-impact summaries (varying with education); question-form headings affected judged familiarity and reliability, not reliable comprehension gains. Do not force "you" or question headings mechanically.
- **Redish and Selzer, Technical Communication 32(4), 1985, pp. 46–52**: a formula that counts sentence length and word length is a weak predictor for adult technical text. A good readability score does not show that readers can understand or use the text, and setting a score as a requirement pushes writers to write to the formula instead of the reader.
- **Connatser 1999** (DOI 10.2190/6EWH-J5C5-AV1X-KDGJ, abstract): text difficulty is a reader's perception and cannot be calculated by counting syllables, word length, and sentence length.

## Minimal Manual & error recovery

- **Carroll, Smith-Kerker, Ford, and Mazur-Rimetz, "The Minimal Manual," Human-Computer Interaction 3(2), 1987–1988** (DOI 10.1207/s15327051hci0302_2, abstract): across two experiments (51 office workers), the minimal manual was briefer, helped learners coordinate attention between system and manual, trained error recognition and recovery, and supported later lookup.
- **Black, Carroll, and McGuigan, CHI 1987** (DOI 10.1145/29933.275623): training minutes across four manuals were inferential (104), skeletal (109), rehearsal (121), and lengthy (128). Brevity without the error-recovery check is not the manual that won.
